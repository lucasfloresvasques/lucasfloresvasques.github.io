The 2026 runoff, municipality by municipality

On the night of 25 October 2026 I followed the count of Brazil's presidential runoff, between Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro, with a model that projects the result from the municipalities already counted. It ran 175 times between 17:08 and 21:28 (Brasília time), each time reading the results of all 5,571 municipalities from the website of the Superior Electoral Court (TSE).

The count swung. Flávio Bolsonaro took the lead at 19:46, with 14% of polling sections counted, and Lula only regained it at 20:25. The projection held steady. With 2% of sections counted, it had almost nailed the result, within the margin; with 8%, it had nailed Lula's win. At the final reading, with 99.88% of sections counted, Lula had 51.76% of the valid votes cast for the two candidates; the projection said 51.78%.

Transcript of the narration (English translation)

2022 and 2026. What changed? And how did Data Lucas read the night of the count?

In 2022, Lula beat Jair Bolsonaro by 2.1 million votes. In 2026, against Flávio Bolsonaro, his lead doubled: 4.4 million.

Lula gained in 16 states and lost ground in 11. Amapá swung to Lula; Tocantins, to Flávio.

In the cities, a paradox. Lula lost ground in most municipalities, but grew in the large ones: he gained in 73 of the 75 largest.

From the first to the second round, almost 13% more votes. And the more a city turned out, the more Lula grew.

Abroad, Lula also gained: from 51% to 53%.

As the votes were counted, the tally swung. With 7% of polling sections counted, the panel predicted: Flávio would take the lead, and Lula would regain it near 40%. That is what happened, at 14% and 47%. And the projection never moved: always Lula.

With 2% of sections counted, the projection was off by less than one point. At the end, by two hundredths of a point. In all 144 rounds, the result stayed within the margin.

The polls had Lula losing. Data Lucas called Lula the winner with only 2.7% of sections counted. At 8%, it was decided.

Data Lucas.

Election night

The average of the polls from the day before, as recorded by the panel, gave Lula 49.3% of the valid vote, that is, a defeat. With 2.2% of sections counted, the projection was already at 50.95%, 0.82 points from the result and within the margin. At 19:22, with 2.7% counted, the panel began to call Lula the winner. At 19:35, with 8.1%, Lula's projected lead exceeded the margin even after allowing for the worst possible bias from the order in which votes arrived; the win was nailed down. From 10% of sections onwards, the projection never moved more than 0.45 points away from the result. The raw count only came within half a point of the result at 80.8% counted, at 20:54. At 21:09, with 96.8% of sections in, Lula's lead in the count exceeded the estimate of the votes still to come.

What changed since 2022

Lula went from 50.90% in 2022 to 51.76% in 2026, and his lead in votes grew from 2.14 million to about 4.37 million. He gained in 16 of the 27 federative units and lost ground in 11. Two states changed sides, Amapá to Lula and Tocantins to Flávio Bolsonaro.

Across municipalities, the movement split by size. Lula lost ground in 4,352 of the 5,562 comparable municipalities, almost all of them small, and gained in 73 of the 75 with 200,000 votes or more. Between the two rounds, the more the valid vote for the two finalists grew in a city, the more Lula grew there; in 2022 he had lost ground at every level of growth.

How the projection works

The starting point is the 2026 first round in each municipality. For every municipality already counted, the model measures how much Lula rose or fell relative to the first round, between the two finalists, on a logistic scale. From these measures it estimates a shift for each state, pulled towards the national average while the state has few votes counted. It also estimates a common slope, which lets more and less pro-Lula municipalities move differently, and the relative turnout of each state.

The votes still to come in each municipality get the first-round baseline plus the estimated shift. Municipalities with more of their vote counted weigh more in the estimation, with weights proportional to votes and to the fourth power of the share counted. This guards against order bias within a city, since the sections that arrive first may not represent those still missing.

The panel only called a winner when the projected margin exceeded the error band from the 2022 test.

The test with the 2022 election

Before the election I tested the model on 2022, using that year's first round as the baseline and the runoff as the target (Lula 50.90%). Since there is no historical record of the order in which votes arrive, I simulated five orders. These were random, regional (South, Southeast and Centre-West first), regional with large cities delayed, regional with bias within cities, and a worst case in which the zones most favourable to the opponent arrive first.
